Research

EU AI Act & GDPR Alignment
LLB Dissertation · 10,000 words · 2024

"The EU AI Act represents the world's first comprehensive attempt to regulate artificial intelligence by risk — but its interaction with GDPR creates significant compliance complexity that existing frameworks are ill-equipped to resolve."

This dissertation examines the regulatory architecture established by the EU AI Act and its alignment — and points of tension — with the General Data Protection Regulation. The analysis focuses on four prohibited AI practices: biometric mass surveillance, social scoring systems, manipulative AI techniques, and exploitation of vulnerable groups, evaluating how each interacts with GDPR's established rights framework.

The research maps the Act's risk-classification system (unacceptable, high, limited, minimal) against existing data protection obligations, identifying where the two regimes complement each other and where they produce conflicting compliance requirements. Special attention is given to the fundamental rights implications of algorithmic decision-making and the accountability gaps that emerge when AI outputs affect legal interests without triggering clear regulatory oversight.

Cross-border compliance is a recurring theme — particularly the UK's regulatory divergence post-Brexit, which creates a fragmented landscape for AI deployment across the Channel. The dissertation concludes with an assessment of the Act's adequacy as a framework for governing responsible AI in data-intensive environments, and the governance questions it leaves unresolved.

// Key Findings
  • GDPR's data minimisation principle conflicts with AI systems requiring large training datasets — the Act provides no clear resolution mechanism
  • Biometric surveillance prohibitions create compliance uncertainty for facial recognition deployed in nominally private contexts
  • Social scoring prohibitions apply only to public authorities — private sector equivalents remain largely ungoverned
  • The Act's risk tiers do not map cleanly to GDPR's special category data protections, creating dual-compliance overhead
  • UK post-Brexit divergence means EU-based AI providers face asymmetric obligations when operating across the Channel
  • Algorithmic accountability obligations remain insufficiently defined to support meaningful legal challenge by affected individuals
